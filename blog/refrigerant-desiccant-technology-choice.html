Refrigerant or desiccant: the technology decision.

Two completely different ways to take water out of air. The right one is almost always obvious from ambient temperature and target RH — and almost never from "how many litres per day do I need."

The two cycles

A refrigerant dehumidifier moves heat. A compressor lowers refrigerant pressure on the cold side, letting it evaporate at 4–8°C; air drawn across the coil deposits moisture as condensate. The compressed hot refrigerant on the other side reheats the dry air before it returns to the room. Net effect: dry air at near room temperature, with a small net heat gain.

A desiccant dehumidifier moves moisture. A rotating wheel of silica honeycomb passes through the process air stream, adsorbing water vapour directly from the air. The wheel continues into a reactivation air stream warmed by a PTC heater; the heat drives water off the wheel and out a reactivation exhaust. The wheel returns to the process side dry. Net effect: dry air, slight temperature gain on process, significant warm wet exhaust on reactivation.

Where each one shines

Refrigerant wins on cost-per-litre wherever air temperature is high enough to make condensation efficient. Roughly:

ConditionsRefrigerantDesiccant
25–35°C / 60–95% RH (tropical residential, commercial)BestWasteful
15–25°C / 50–70% RH (temperate office, gallery)StrongAcceptable
10–15°C / 40–60% RH (wine cellar, mild cold store)BorderlineStrong
0–10°C / 30–50% RH (cold storage, food processing)Inefficient or unusableBest
Below 0°C (freezer rooms, lyophilisation)UnusableOnly option
Any temperature targeting below 30% RHCannot reachRequired

The lines blur in the middle of the table. Below 10°C, frost forms on the refrigerant coil; the unit spends as much time defrosting as drying. Below 0°C, defrost cycles dominate completely and effective capacity collapses to near zero.

Capacity per kilowatt

At 25°C / 60% RH, a refrigerant unit removes roughly 2.5–3.0 litres of water per kWh of electrical input. A desiccant unit at the same conditions removes 0.8–1.2 litres per kWh — about a third the efficiency. That is why refrigerant dominates the 90% of the market where conditions are warm.

At 0°C / 40% RH, a refrigerant unit removes maybe 0.3 litres per kWh (most of its energy goes to defrost). A desiccant unit removes 0.6–0.8 litres per kWh. Same desiccant unit, completely different efficiency story — because the alternative is failing entirely.

The hybrid case

For large industrial dry rooms (lithium battery production, pharmaceutical packing, freeze-drying), the most efficient setup combines both technologies. A refrigerant pre-dryer drops the incoming air from ambient (say 30°C / 80% RH) down to 50% RH — easy work for the refrigerant unit. A desiccant stage takes that from 50% RH down to the process target of 1–5% RH — easy work for the desiccant unit. Each runs in its sweet spot. Total energy is lower than either alone.

The DBA range supports this directly: an industrial GE-series unit upstream of a DD-series desiccant, often with a humidity control HC unit handling final polishing. These are designed as projects, not catalogue purchases — share the parameters and we design the system around them.

Common decisions

  • Hotel ballroom in Dubai (28°C / 65% RH target): refrigerant. DBA-UTC120 ceiling units or GEC145LD-HP for larger spaces.
  • Wine cellar in Bordeaux (14°C / 60% RH target): refrigerant + humidity control. DBA-HC68 with cold-rated package.
  • Cold storage warehouse in Rotterdam (2°C / 50% RH): desiccant. DBA-DD550 or DD850 with refrigerant pre-treatment.
  • Lithium battery dry room in Seoul (22°C / 1% RH): hybrid system with desiccant as primary. DBA-DD850 as core, with project-specific air handling.
  • Hospital pharmacy in Mumbai (22°C / 35% RH): refrigerant with low-grain LGR variant. DBA-GEX series adapted for fixed install.

The decision rule

If conditions sit comfortably above 10°C and target RH stays above 35%, choose refrigerant. Otherwise look at desiccant. The crossover band 5–15°C with target RH 35–50% is where it pays to do the energy maths for your specific case.
