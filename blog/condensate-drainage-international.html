Condensate drainage: head, pumps, and code compliance.

A 145 L/day commercial ceiling unit can produce six litres an hour at peak. The drainage path needs to be as carefully designed as the duct path. Get either wrong and you have built a leak.

The three patterns

Every ceiling install drains by one of three routes.

  • Direct gravity drop: the unit is above a bathroom, kitchen, or service space with an existing trap or floor drain immediately below. Cleanest route. Use it when available.
  • Pump and traverse: the unit's integrated condensate pump lifts above the ceiling and pushes the line laterally to a remote drop point.
  • Pump to external tank: for long lifts or high flows, the unit's pump feeds a small tank with its own larger pump, which handles the building-side lift to a sanitary stack.

These ratings are total head — vertical lift plus friction in the horizontal run plus bend losses. Budget all three when planning the line.

Calculating friction

For the standard 16 mm internal-diameter clear hose supplied with most ceiling units:

  • Horizontal run: roughly 5 cm of head per metre at typical condensate flow
  • Each 90° elbow: 10 cm of head
  • Each tee fitting (joining lines): 15 cm of head

A worked example: a GEC145LD-HP with 1.8 m pump head, 2 m vertical lift, 6 m horizontal traverse, three 90° elbows.

Total head budget: 2.0 + (6 × 0.05) + (3 × 0.1) = 2.6 m. Pump head 1.8 m. Overloaded. Either reduce the lift, use a larger-bore hose (drops friction), or specify an external pump tank.

Code requirements by region

Condensate discharge from HVAC and dehumidification equipment is regulated by local plumbing code. The most common requirements:

  • EU (EN 12056): indirect discharge to a building drain. Air gap recommended; trap mandatory if connected to soil stack.
  • USA (UPC / IPC): air gap mandatory to building drain. Some jurisdictions require pH neutralization between the unit and PVC drain to protect concrete in slab penetrations. Condensate neutralizers are inexpensive, listed for purpose, and pass inspection.
  • UK (BS EN 16798): indirect discharge with trap to gully or soil pipe.
  • UAE / KSA (SBC / mechanical code): drain via floor drain or grease trap; air gap and trap for commercial.
  • Singapore (PUB): drain via floor trap or sanitary stack with trap and air gap; commercial requires plan check.

When in doubt, route to a fixture drain (lavatory, mop sink) via air gap. That detail passes inspection in almost every jurisdiction.

Auxiliary protection

For any install above a finished ceiling, two extra elements pay back many times their cost:

  • Water-leak sensor — wired to the unit's leak port if available, or to a standalone alarm. DBA's UTC, GEC, and HC ranges all have a dry-contact input for this.
  • Secondary drip pan — a small metal or plastic pan under the unit, with its own drain or sensor. Catches a failed primary pump before the ceiling does.

Insulation

In humid climates the drain line will sweat. Insulate the first 1–2 metres of horizontal run with closed-cell foam to avoid drip damage to adjacent ceiling structure. In cold climates, freeze protection becomes the concern for any drain section that exits the conditioned envelope — heat trace or relocate the line indoors.

The 20% margin

Design every drain line so the calculated head is no more than 80% of the pump's rated head. The remaining 20% covers filter loading, biofilm growth in the line, and pump wear. Skip the margin and you are designing for a perfect day on commissioning, not for a working unit at year three.
